# Five Cyber Hygiene Habits Every Student Should Practise

Your phone, laptop and online accounts hold more than entertainment. They may contain class notes, assignments, photos, personal messages and access to important services. Losing access to an account or device can interrupt studies and expose information you did not intend to share. Protecting your digital life does not require becoming a cybersecurity expert. A few consistent habits can reduce avoidable risks.

This guide focuses on five everyday behaviours: installing updates safely, keeping recoverable backups, reviewing app permissions, treating public Wi-Fi carefully and checking links before opening them. None of these habits guarantees complete protection. Together, however, they make common mistakes less likely and help you recover more effectively when something goes wrong.

## 1. Update your devices through trusted channels

Operating systems, browsers and apps receive updates for different reasons. Some add features or fix bugs; others address security vulnerabilities. Delaying important updates can leave known weaknesses unpatched, especially when a device is connected to the internet and used for coursework, messaging or financial services.

Turn on automatic updates where appropriate, and periodically check your operating system, browser and frequently used apps. Install updates through the device's built-in settings or the publisher's official app store or website. If a notification appears unexpectedly in a browser window and claims that your device is infected or urgently needs a download, do not follow its link. Close the message and check for updates independently through the normal settings menu.

Updates can take time, so schedule restarts when convenient rather than postponing them indefinitely. Keep enough free storage for updates to install correctly. If you use a shared or college-managed device, follow the institution's update policy and ask its support team when you are unsure.

**Try this:** Open your device's update settings and check whether automatic updates are enabled.

## 2. Back up important work—and test it

Imagine losing your laptop shortly before an assignment deadline. If the only copy of your project was stored on that laptop, recovery may be difficult or impossible. A backup is a separate copy that helps you restore files after accidental deletion, device failure, loss or corruption.

Identify the files that would be difficult to recreate: assignments, project folders, notes, photos and important documents. Keep another copy in a suitable location, such as an external drive or a reputable cloud backup service. For important data, avoid depending on one copy that is continuously exposed to the same device, account or failure. Protect cloud accounts with strong, unique passwords and multi-factor authentication where available.

Do not assume that synchronisation is identical to backup. A synchronised folder may copy additions, edits and deletions between devices. If a file is deleted or changed accidentally, that change may also appear elsewhere. Some services provide version history or a recovery window, but the details differ by provider and plan. Understand the recovery features of the service you use.

A backup is not fully useful until you know you can restore from it. Occasionally open a backed-up file or test the restore process using a non-critical sample. Keep external backup drives disconnected when they are not being used, where practical, so that a problem affecting the computer does not automatically affect every copy.

**Try this:** Back up one important assignment folder and check that at least one file can be opened from the backup.

## 3. Question app permissions

Apps may request access to your camera, microphone, location, contacts, photos, files or other device features. Some permissions are necessary for an app to work: a navigation app needs location access for turn-by-turn directions, for example. Other requests may be unrelated to the feature you want to use.

Before granting access, ask a simple question: does this permission make sense for the feature I am using? A photo-editing app may need access to selected photos, but it may not need continuous location access. A basic calculator generally has no obvious reason to access your contacts. These are prompts to review the request, not automatic proof that an app is malicious.

Where your device offers the option, prefer limited, one-time or “while using the app” access instead of permanent access. You can also deny a permission initially and see whether the feature still works. Review permissions periodically, especially after installing an unfamiliar app or when an app changes its features. Remove permissions that are no longer needed.

Use official app stores where possible, check the developer and app details, and be cautious about apps that request broad access without a clear explanation. The exact permission controls and menu names vary across Android versions, iOS versions and device manufacturers.

**Try this:** Open your app-permission settings and review location, camera and microphone access.

## 4. Be careful on public Wi-Fi

Free Wi-Fi at a café, railway station, airport or campus can be convenient. However, you may not know who operates the network or whether a similarly named hotspot is genuine. A network name that looks familiar is not enough to establish that it belongs to the venue.

Ask staff or check the venue's official information for the correct network name. Keep your device and browser updated, and avoid sensitive transactions when you cannot establish that the network and destination are trustworthy. Be especially careful with unexpected sign-in pages and requests for account credentials. When possible, use your mobile connection for sensitive activity on a network you do not trust.

HTTPS helps encrypt the connection between your browser and the domain shown in the address bar. It does not prove that the website operator is honest. A deceptive website can also use HTTPS, so inspect the domain and the context before entering information.

A reputable virtual private network (VPN) can protect some network traffic between your device and the VPN provider, depending on its configuration and the service. It does not make a fraudulent website legitimate, guarantee anonymity or protect you from every threat. Continue checking links, using strong authentication and keeping your device updated.

**Try this:** Before joining a public hotspot, confirm its exact name with the venue.

## 5. Check links before you open them

A message may appear to come from a delivery company, bank, college, social platform or friend. It may claim that an account will close, a parcel is delayed or a prize is waiting. Urgency, unexpected attachments, unusual payment requests and requests to sign in through a message link are reasons to pause.

Inspect the actual domain rather than relying only on the displayed organisation name, logo or link text. Look for misspellings, extra words, misleading subdomains and unfamiliar domain endings. On a computer, you can often preview a link destination without opening it; on a phone, press-and-hold behaviour varies, so use caution and do not open a link just to inspect it.

For important services, open the official app or type a known website address yourself instead of following an unexpected sign-in link. If a message claims to be from someone you know and asks for money or sensitive information, verify through another communication channel.

The padlock or HTTPS indicator is useful, but it is not a trust certificate. It indicates that the connection to the named domain is encrypted. It does not confirm that the domain belongs to the organisation you expect or that the site is safe to use.

**Try this:** When you receive an unexpected account alert, navigate to the service independently instead of using the message link.

## Make the habits routine

Choose one small action today: enable updates, review app permissions, back up an assignment folder or remove an unfamiliar saved Wi-Fi network. Repeat these checks periodically and whenever you install a new app, change devices or notice an unexpected account message.

Cyber hygiene is not a one-time installation and cannot prevent every attack. It is a practical routine that reduces avoidable exposure and improves your ability to recover. When something seems unusual, pause, verify through a trusted channel and ask for help rather than rushing.

**Remember:** Update through trusted channels. Keep recoverable backups. Limit permissions to genuine needs. Treat public networks carefully. Check links and domains before sharing information.
